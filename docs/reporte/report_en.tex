\begin{abstract}
Small importers who sell iPhones in pesos in Colombia and pay for them in dollars in the United States, shipping them through a parcel-forwarding service, bear the risk that the peso--dollar exchange rate (TRM) changes between the sale and the payment, without the analytical tools of large firms. We present \emph{TRM Predict}, a cloud-native decision-support system that forecasts, for the next business day, the direction of the TRM, its expected value and the probability of a large move, and shows the cost in pesos of an iPhone today versus tomorrow with a purchase recommendation. The project followed Design Thinking as its overall methodology and KDD/CRISP-DM in the prototype phase. The pipeline integrates the official TRM, eight market series, two crypto-assets and a web-scraped news-sentiment index into a leakage-free data set of 2{,}159 days and 43 features. Unsupervised mining identifies, with K-Means and the elbow method ($k=4$, silhouette 0.116), four market regimes (calm, risk-on, risk-off and crisis), 57 anomalous days and association rules with lift up to 1.24. Among 19 tuned models, only the ensembles beat chance out of sample. The selected Random Forest reaches a test ROC-AUC of 0.555 for direction and 0.585 for risk, and a regression RMSE 1.3\% below the random walk, a modest predictive ability. An importer rule (wait if the probability of a fall is at least 55\% outside the crisis regime) recommended waiting on 101 of 432 test days and was right on 63.4\% of them, although the period was one of peso appreciation in which waiting at random also saved money. The system runs on AWS and is fully described in Terraform (64 resources): serverless ingestion, an S3 data lake replicated across regions, PostgreSQL on RDS and a containerized API behind a load balancer with auto-scaling in two availability zones. Experiments show that, with at least one instance per zone, terminating a server causes no failed request (100\% availability versus 77.8\% with a single minimum instance), and that scaling out from two to four instances raises throughput by 84\% and cuts median latency by 60\%. The project contributes to SDG~8 (decent work and economic growth) and SDG~9 (industry, innovation and infrastructure).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The \emph{Tasa Representativa del Mercado} (TRM) is the official COP/USD exchange rate certified every day by Colombia's Financial Superintendence. It sets the price in pesos of imported goods and the cost of dollar-denominated debt. Between September 2017 and October 2026 it moved between 2{,}705 (April 2018) and 5{,}061 COP/USD (November 2022); during 2025 alone it ranged from 3{,}707 to 4{,}417, a 19\% swing, and 18\% of trading days in our sample show a variation larger than 1\%.

Large firms hedge this risk with treasury desks and derivatives, whereas a small iPhone importer usually decides by intuition. The importer quotes or sells the device in pesos, but pays for it in dollars at a US store and ships it through a parcel-forwarding service: if the TRM rises between the day of the sale and the day of the payment, the margin shrinks. The practical question is: do I buy today or wait until tomorrow? The project therefore addresses the 2030 Agenda~\cite{un2015}: SDG~8 (decent work and economic growth, by giving small businesses a tool to manage exchange-rate risk) and SDG~9 (industry, innovation and infrastructure, through a scalable cloud infrastructure).

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A reproducible KDD/CRISP-DM pipeline, framed within a Design Thinking process~\cite{brown2008}, over heterogeneous public sources, including a purpose-built web scraper; (ii) an honest benchmark of 19 regression and classification models against random-walk and majority-class baselines, with SHAP and LIME explanations and a decision-oriented evaluation; (iii) a production-style AWS deployment written entirely as Infrastructure as Code, with measured scalability, availability and cost.

\section{Problem Statement}
\textbf{Users.} Small importers who sell iPhones in pesos in Colombia and pay for them in dollars in the United States, using a parcel-forwarding service. Their problem with the TRM is the gap between the day they set the price in pesos and the day they pay in dollars, plus the surcharge the bank charges over the official TRM. \textbf{Question.} Using only public data available before the next TRM is published, can we estimate (a) the probability that it rises, (b) its expected value and (c) the probability of an abnormally large move, verifiably better than naive baselines, and explain each prediction? Exchange rates are notoriously hard to forecast: Meese and Rogoff showed that structural models fail to beat a random walk out of sample~\cite{meese1983}. Consequently, the random walk and the majority class are mandatory baselines in this work.

\section{Related Work}
Henrique \emph{et al.}~\cite{henrique2019} review machine learning for financial-market prediction and report the dominance of support vector machines and neural networks, with ensembles gaining ground; Sezer \emph{et al.}~\cite{sezer2020} survey deep learning for financial time series and stress its weak interpretability and reproducibility. Most studies address major currency pairs or equity indices and stop at offline evaluation. This work targets an emerging-market currency, uses country-specific drivers (oil, peer currencies, local news), favors interpretable ensembles explained with SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017} and LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016}, and delivers a deployed, reproducible service.

\section{System Overview}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} depicts the system. Every business day at 18:30 (Colombia time), an EventBridge rule triggers the \emph{ingestion} Lambda, which calls the TRM open-data API, Yahoo Finance and Binance, scrapes \emph{La Rep\'ublica}, and writes one JSON bundle to the raw zone of an S3 data lake. That object triggers the \emph{ETL} Lambda, which upserts the data into PostgreSQL. Models are trained offline with the pipeline described below and published to S3. A FastAPI service in Docker serves predictions, what-if scenarios and a web dashboard (Fig.~\ref{fig:dash}).

Table~\ref{tab:sources} lists the sources. It uses only the Python standard library so that it runs inside Lambda, issues one request per section per day and isolates its failures from numeric ingestion. Because historical headlines are not available, the score is stored daily and shown in the dashboard; it will enter the models once enough history accumulates.

\subsection{Preparation and Data Quality}
Cleaning includes type casting, removal of duplicates and invalid dates, positivity checks and an audit of outlying returns with the $3\times$IQR rule (e.g., 61 for Brent and 44 for the US 10Y yield; they are genuine market events and were kept, as tree ensembles are robust to them). Integration uses a strict \texttt{merge\_asof} with \texttt{allow\_exact\_matches=False}: for the TRM valid on day $d$, only market closes \emph{before} $d$ are used, which prevents look-ahead leakage. The final data set has 2{,}159 days (Sep.~2017--Oct.~2026), constrained by the start of the crypto series, and 43 features (TRM log-returns and lags, 5/20-day volatility, moving-average ratio, z-score, calendar; 1/5-day returns and 20-day volatility of each market; and the spot--TRM gap). Targets are the next log-return $y_{ret}$, its sign $y_{up}$ and a large-move flag $y_{big}=\mathbb{1}[|y_{ret}|>q_{75}]$, with $q_{75}=0.86\%$ computed on the training set only.

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}
TRM returns are leptokurtic (excess kurtosis 3.0, skewness 0.50, Jarque--Bera $p<10^{-100}$) and show positive lag-1 autocorrelation (0.17), consistent with the TRM being a volume-weighted daily average. Only 48.3\% of days are up-moves. The variables most correlated with the next return are the last TRM return (0.17), Brent (0.13) and the DXY (0.09). Rolling correlations (Fig.~\ref{fig:eda}) reveal that these relations change sign over time, which anticipates the low out-of-sample predictability.

\subsection{Feature Engineering and PCA}
PCA on standardized features needs 26 of 43 components to retain 90\% of the variance, so the features are only weakly redundant and dimensionality reduction would discard information; it is used for analysis and visualization only. PC1 loads on peso and peer-currency momentum (5-day TRM return, USD/COP, USD/MXN, z-score), and PC2 on global volatility (S\&P~500, US~10Y, Brent, DXY).

\subsection{Clustering: Market Regimes}
Nine regime features (volatility and 5-day momentum of the TRM, VIX, DXY, Brent, BTC and S\&P~500) were standardized. Tails were clipped to the 1st--99th percentiles before K-Means (and Ward hierarchical clustering as a contrast), whereas DBSCAN~\cite{ester1996} was applied to the unclipped data as an anomaly detector. The elbow method selects $k=4$ (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}): a calm regime (752 days, the TRM rises on 50.8\% of the next days), a risk-on regime in which the peso appreciates (754 days, 43.8\%), a risk-off regime in which the dollar rises (606 days, 51.3\%) and a crisis or high-volatility regime (47 days, concentrated in 2020) with the highest standard deviation of the next return (1.47\%). The low silhouette (0.116; Davies--Bouldin 1.91) shows overlapping regimes, which are thus used as context rather than as hard classes. DBSCAN ($\varepsilon=2.89$ from the 95th percentile of the 18-distance curve, \texttt{min\_samples}=18) flags 57 anomalous days (45 in 2020), on which next-day volatility is 1.73 times that of normal days.

\subsection{Association Rules}
Daily returns of eight markets were discretized into terciles (\emph{up}/\emph{down}), together with a high-volatility flag and the next TRM direction. Apriori~\cite{agrawal1994} and FP-Growth~\cite{han2000} (minimum support 0.05, lift $\geq$ 1.05) produce the same 498 frequent itemsets and 91 rules whose consequent is the TRM direction, which cross-validates both implementations. Table~\ref{tab:rules} shows the strongest ones, which agree with economic intuition: oil up with a weak global dollar anticipates a peso appreciation, and joint depreciation of peer currencies with rising VIX anticipates a TRM rise.

\section{Machine Learning}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Data were split chronologically: training Sep.~2017--Dec.~2024 (1{,}727 days) and test Dec.~2024--Oct.~2026 (432 days). Inside training, hyperparameters were tuned with 5-fold \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}, so each validation fold lies after its training fold. All models are scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011} pipelines. Regression candidates: linear, polynomial (degree 2 on the $k$ best features) + Ridge, Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net, Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001} and Gradient Boosting~\cite{friedman2001}. Classification candidates (for direction and risk): logistic regression, KNN, SVM (RBF), Random Forest, AdaBoost~\cite{freund1997} and Gradient Boosting. Grid Search was used for the low-dimensional spaces (linear models, KNN, SVM, AdaBoost) and Random Search~\cite{bergstra2012} with 15 draws for the ensembles. Final models are chosen by the cross-validation score, never by the test score.

\subsection{Results}
Tables~\ref{tab:clf} and~\ref{tab:reg} report the test metrics. In direction classification, linear models, KNN and SVM fall at or below chance (AUC 0.47--0.50) even though their cross-validation AUC was about 0.56: their apparent signal did not survive the change of period. Only the ensembles keep a positive edge (AUC 0.54--0.57), which indicates non-linear interactions among drivers. The Random Forest, selected by its cross-validation AUC (0.582), reaches a test AUC of 0.555, an accuracy of 53.2\% and an F1 of 0.514; Gradient Boosting had a slightly higher test AUC (0.566). For risk (an alert for a move larger than 0.86\%), the Random Forest reaches a test AUC of 0.585. In regression, the Random Forest has an RMSE of 0.00759 versus 0.00769 for the random walk (1.3\% lower) and an $R^2$ of 0.017. Direction accuracy at the 0.5 threshold is still below the majority class (54.6\%, a period in which the peso appreciated), a limitation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

\subsection{Interpretability}
TreeSHAP on the Random Forest direction model (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}) ranks the spot--TRM gap first: when the last spot quote is above the current TRM, the next TRM tends to rise, which reflects how the TRM averages the trades of the day. It is followed by the last TRM return, the daily change of the DXY and of the USD/COP spot, and the 5-day Brent return. Impurity-based feature importance agrees on four of the top five variables (spot--TRM gap, last TRM return, USD/COP and DXY daily changes). LIME on the last test day gives a local explanation: the 5\% five-day rise of Brent and the 1\% previous-day fall of the USD/COP spot lower the probability of a rise, while the spot--TRM gap and the last TRM return raise it, a readable explanation that can be shown next to each forecast.

\subsection{Decision-Oriented Evaluation}
Statistical metrics do not say whether the model helps a user. The web page puts the question ``do I buy the iPhone today or wait until tomorrow?'' at its center: the importer picks an iPhone model and gets its cost in pesos at today's TRM and at the TRM expected for tomorrow, adding the parcel-forwarding fee, the bank's surcharge over the TRM and taxes to Apple's dollar price (example parameters kept in a configuration file). The rule recommends \emph{waiting} if $P(\text{down})\geq 55\%$ and the day's K-Means regime is not Crisis; otherwise, \emph{buying today}. In the backtest (Table~\ref{tab:regla}), on every test day the importer must pay USD~1{,}000 and decides whether to pay today or wait until the next day. The rule recommended waiting on 101 of 432 days and was right 63.4\% of those times; averaged over all days, it saved 2{,}314.7 COP per USD~1{,}000, versus 1{,}383.3 COP for waiting at random (1{,}000 simulations) and 12{,}701.5 COP for an oracle that knows the future. The result must be read with caution: the test period was one of peso appreciation, so waiting at random also saved money. For the risk alert, a probability threshold chosen on the last training fold so that the alert rate matches the historical frequency yields a test precision of 30.1\% versus a base rate of 22.5\% (lift 1.34) with 25.8\% recall. The Random Forest probabilities barely improve on the calibration of the base rate (Brier 0.2474 vs.\ 0.2492).

\subsection{Model Selection}
Random Forest was selected for direction, risk and regression because it has the best cross-validation score in two of the three tasks, it is never far from the best test result, and a single model family simplifies maintenance and explanation. The randomized search of each ensemble takes 60--77~s on a laptop, and serving the three models takes about 0.5~s per request on a \texttt{t3.micro}. Gradient Boosting is kept as a documented alternative, as it reached a slightly higher test AUC for direction (0.566), but it was not selected because selection relies on cross-validation.

\subsection{Infrastructure as Code}
All infrastructure is declared in Terraform~\cite{terraform_aws} (10 files, 64 managed resources): VPC with public and private subnets in two availability zones, security groups, a free S3 gateway endpoint that replaces a NAT gateway, the versioned and encrypted data lake with lifecycle rules, cross-region replication, ECR, RDS, SSM parameters, ALB, launch template, Auto Scaling Group and target-tracking policy, both Lambdas with their triggers, CloudWatch alarms and dashboard, and a monthly AWS Budget. A single \texttt{terraform apply} creates the stack in about 15 minutes, most of them for RDS, and \texttt{terraform destroy} removes it. Variables allow the same code to run in AWS Academy, whose \texttt{LabRole} replaces the IAM roles that Terraform would otherwise create.

\subsection{Security}
RDS and the ETL Lambda live in private subnets without Internet egress; the database accepts connections only from the API and the ETL security groups; API instances accept traffic only from the ALB and require IMDSv2; the database password is generated by Terraform and stored encrypted in SSM Parameter Store; S3 blocks public access and encrypts at rest. No personal data are processed, so no informed consent is required.

\subsection{Scalability and High Availability: Experiments}
Two experiments were run against the deployed stack from a client in Colombia (Table~\ref{tab:exp}). Without load, \texttt{/health} answers in 197~ms (mostly network round trip), \texttt{/api/predict} in 709~ms and \texttt{/api/scenario} in 982~ms.

\emph{High availability.} A probe called \texttt{/health} every 0.5~s and one instance was terminated at $t=30$~s (Fig.~\ref{fig:ha}). In the first run the auto-scaling group had a minimum of one instance: because the idle group had already scaled in to a single server, terminating it left the load balancer without targets for about 109~s, until a replacement passed its health checks (availability 77.8\%). After raising the minimum to two (one per zone), the same test produced 378 successful requests out of 378; the load balancer drained the failed target and the group restored full capacity in 139~s. The lesson is that high availability depends on the scaling policy as much as on the multi-AZ topology.

\emph{Scalability.} Sixteen concurrent clients sent \texttt{POST /api/scenario} requests, the most CPU-intensive endpoint, for 14~min (Fig.~\ref{fig:load}). Target tracking at 60\% CPU raised the desired capacity from two to four at minute 6.6, and the new instances were healthy at minute 9.2. Throughput rose from 4.1 to 7.6 requests/s (+84\%) and the median latency fell from 4.0 to 1.6~s, with no errors in 4{,}543 requests. The roughly 2.5~min between the scaling decision and the new capacity is dominated by instance boot and image download, which a warm pool or a smaller image would shorten.

\subsection{Disaster Recovery}
S3 versioning protects against accidental deletion, and cross-region replication copied the 1.6~MB historical bundle and the 12~MB model to us-west-2 within minutes. RDS keeps seven days of automated backups with point-in-time recovery. Since all data can be re-ingested from public sources and all infrastructure is code, the strategy is \emph{backup and restore}~\cite{aws_wellarchitected} with RPO $\approx$ 24~h and RTO $<$ 1~h: \texttt{terraform apply} with \texttt{region=us-west-2} recreates the stack and the backfill reloads the database in about 2 minutes (14~s ingestion and 109~s ETL measured).

\subsection{Cost Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:cost} compares the on-demand estimate with the observed usage. The dominant costs are the ALB, the public IPv4 addresses and the always-on EC2 and RDS instances; S3, Lambda, EventBridge and SSM are negligible. Avoiding a NAT gateway saves about USD~33 per month. Because the stack is created and destroyed with Terraform, the expected cost of the whole semester (about 40 development hours, one day of tests and one week of operation for the defense) is about USD~21 for the whole semester, versus about USD~63.6 per month if left running 24/7. \section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc}
The results confirm the difficulty documented since Meese and Rogoff~\cite{meese1983}: the predictable component of daily FX moves is small and unstable. Three lessons follow. First, cross-validation alone was misleading: every model had a CV AUC near 0.56--0.58, but only the ensembles kept an edge on a later, unseen period; a strictly chronological test set was essential. Second, the most useful feature (spot--TRM gap) comes from domain understanding of how the TRM is computed rather than from model complexity. Third, decision-oriented metrics make modest statistical gains interpretable for users. Limitations: the test period was dominated by peso appreciation, so accuracy at the 0.5 threshold is below the majority class and waiting at random also saved money; the model predicts the TRM, not the iPhone price, and the forwarding fee, bank surcharge and taxes are example parameters; validation with real importers is pending; the news-sentiment index lacks history; Yahoo Finance is an unofficial source; and the 2017 start date of crypto data shortens the sample.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
TRM Predict integrates a complete data-mining pipeline, an honestly evaluated set of machine-learning models and a scalable, highly available AWS architecture defined as code into one working product aligned with SDGs 8 and 9. Ensembles were the only models with out-of-sample value, albeit modest: the Random Forest improves on the random walk by only 1.3\% in RMSE. The importer rule was right 63.4\% of the times it recommended waiting and saved on average 2{,}314.7 COP per USD~1{,}000 per day, versus 1{,}383.3 COP at random, in a period of peso appreciation. Future work: evaluating the rule in periods of peso depreciation, validating the module with iPhone importers, probability calibration and cost-sensitive thresholds, adding the accumulated sentiment index and the policy-rate differential, walk-forward retraining scheduled in the cloud, and a serverless version of the API to cut the cost below USD~5 per month.
